Verdict

I agree with the core diagnosis, especially the warning against validating v4 only against opponents derived from the same assumptions. However, the proposed plan is too ambitious for a genuine two-hour deadline.

The right approach is still a strategy tournament, but it should be a narrow ablation tournament, not four fresh architectures plus a new opponent framework. The public arena currently exposes tournament, head-to-head, match, replay, and submission views, but the accessible page does not independently confirm the detailed engine and scoring claims in the pasted analysis.

What is strongest in the recommendation
1. Treating v4 as a control is exactly right

“Beat the incumbent, do not defend it” is the most important instruction in the prompt.

The testing field should contain opponents designed from independent principles. Otherwise, v4 may simply be winning against distorted reflections of itself.

2. The suspected 25bb open-shoving problem deserves immediate attention

If the reported diagnostic is correct and v4 really open-shoves roughly 23% to 27% of combinations at 25bb, that is strategically significant.

It does not prove v4 is bad. The shove EV for a hand hh is broadly:

EVjam(h)=F⋅P+(1−F)⋅[E(h)⋅W−(1−E(h))⋅R]EV_{\text{jam}}(h)=F \cdot P +(1-F)\cdot \left[E(h)\cdot W-(1-E(h))\cdot R\right]

where FF is the opponent’s fold probability. A small error in assumed FF can reverse the decision because the amount risked is much larger than the blinds won immediately.

The particularly worrying point is not merely the shove frequency. It is the possibility that v4:

models a shove with a relatively complete terminal EV calculation;
models a small raise with a rougher approximation;
therefore chooses the action that is easier for its code to evaluate, rather than the action that has the highest poker EV.

That is a classic unequal-model-fidelity bug. It should be tested before almost anything else.

3. Paired duplicate deals and diversified opponents are essential

The recommendation correctly recognizes that:

poker results are noisy;
matchups can be non-transitive;
aggregate performance can conceal a catastrophic opponent-specific weakness;
a bot should not be selected from one favorable matchup.

Those are the right experimental principles.

What I would change
1. Do not build A, B, C, and D2 from scratch

That scope is more appropriate for a full day than 120 minutes. Four architectures create too much risk from:

integration defects;
illegal-action handling;
inconsistent state interpretation;
serialization or pickle failures;
insufficient matches per candidate;
last-minute changes that never receive serious testing.

I would limit the first stage to three models:

Model	Purposev4-control	Untouched fallback
v4-preflop	Replace or constrain the opening and facing-raise policies
solid-independent	Small, deterministic 25bb baseline using explicit range tables

Only add adaptive exploitation if one of those models is already stable and clearly competitive.

2. Make D2 the first experiment, not Candidate A

The pasted plan gives Candidate A too much early priority. D2 gives more information per minute.

A clean preflop ablation can answer:

Is v4 succeeding because of its unusual opening model, or despite it?

That is much more diagnostic than immediately replacing every strategic layer.

A useful D2 should not impose a crude global shove cap. It should separate:

button unopened pot;
big blind facing a limp;
big blind facing a small raise;
effective stack depth;
hand class;
opponent fold and call evidence.

At approximately 25bb, open-jamming should normally be an exceptional branch rather than the default alternative to folding or making a normal raise. The exact range remains opponent-dependent and must be tested.

3. Use a minimal solid baseline, not a “complete poker architecture”

Candidate A should be deliberately boring:

explicit 169-class preflop tables;
button actions such as fold, limp if intentionally supported, normal raise, and rare shove;
big-blind call, 3-bet, and 3-bet-jam responses by raise-size bucket;
flop bets around 25% to 33% pot on suitable boards;
larger value and protection bets where appropriate;
equity estimates combined with position, stack-to-pot ratio, and future-action risk;
strict legal-action and timeout protection.

It does not need to approximate a solver. Its job is to reveal whether v4’s complexity is adding value.

4. Postpone Candidate B’s elaborate opponent model

Requiring 15 to 25 observations sounds sensible, but the correct denominator is opportunities, not total hands.

For example:

fold-to-open should use the number of times the opponent faced an open;
fold-to-flop-bet should use the number of times it faced a flop bet;
3-bet rate should use opportunities to 3-bet;
showdown looseness should use observed exposed hands, while acknowledging selection bias.

A simple shrinkage estimate would be safer:

p^=x+αn+α+β\hat p=\frac{x+\alpha}{n+\alpha+\beta}

Then cap strategic adjustment. For example, do not widen a steal range by more than 5 to 10 percentage points until the estimate has substantial support.

But this is a second-stage improvement. Under a hard two-hour deadline, a badly debugged adaptive system is more dangerous than a slightly unexploitive fixed policy.

5. Do not overinterpret 300 deals

Three hundred paired deals may identify crashes or enormous strategic failures, but it will rarely distinguish two reasonably close poker bots reliably.

Use it only for elimination. A practical funnel is:

100 to 150 paired deals: legality, exceptions, timing, gross failures.
300 to 500 paired deals: eliminate clearly weak candidates.
1,000 or more paired deals: compare the final two, if runtime permits.
Inspect both aggregate chips and per-opponent results.

If two finalists remain statistically close, choose the one with:

fewer catastrophic matchups;
lower decision-time variance;
fewer stateful adaptations;
fewer untested branches;
cleaner submission validation.

A better instruction for Claude Code

The existing instruction is strategically thoughtful but long enough that Claude may spend too much time constructing infrastructure and reporting. I would replace it with this more executable version:

We have a hard two-hour submission deadline. Preserve v4 unchanged as the fallback. Do not defend its architecture.

Run only three serious candidates initially:

v4_control: unchanged.
v4_preflop: retain v4 postflop, but replace its preflop opening and facing-raise policy with explicit 25bb stack-aware range tables and normal raise sizing. Do not impose an arbitrary shove cap. Make open-shoving a hand-class and opponent-dependent decision.
solid_baseline: an independent, compact, deterministic 25bb heads-up policy with explicit preflop ranges, small flop bets where appropriate, sensible value betting, and strict legal-action safeguards. Do not copy v4 thresholds.

First inspect and confirm the reported v4 open-shoving behavior using the actual source. Identify whether smaller raises and shoves are being evaluated with unequal model fidelity.

Use the official engine and evaluation harness as the source of truth. Create a compact adversarial field covering tight calling, loose calling, wide 3-betting, frequent shoving, small-bet pressure, an equity-based player, and official dummy opponents.

Use paired duplicate deals and the same opponent set for every candidate:

100 to 150 pairs for smoke testing;
300 to 500 pairs for screening;
at least 1,000 fresh pairs for the final two if runtime permits.

Report aggregate and per-opponent bb/100, confidence intervals if supported, decision faults, illegal actions, timing, and worst matchup. Never select from aggregate bb/100 alone.

Do not build CFR, reinforcement learning, a solver, or a large new framework. Do not add elaborate adaptation until the three initial candidates are valid and compared.

At 105 minutes, freeze strategic changes. The final 15 minutes are reserved for official validation, clean-process loading, serialization, artifact creation, and submission.

The selection rule is:

Reject any candidate with unresolved legality, timing, state, or loading faults.
Prefer clear held-out performance improvements.
If results are inconclusive, choose the candidate with the strongest worst-matchup performance and lowest implementation risk.
If the challenger is not demonstrably safer or stronger, submit v4_control.

Begin with the v4 preflop audit and v4_preflop ablation. Return working files and actual experiment results, not a proposed implementation plan.

Bottom line

The analysis is strategically strong but operationally over-scoped.

My ranking of priorities would be:

Preserve and validate v4.
Run the preflop ablation.
Build one independent solid baseline.
Test all three against diverse opponents.
Only then add a small, bounded exploit layer.

The open-shove finding is the best lead, but it should be treated as an experiment target, not as proof that conventional poker must win. The likely winning move is not four new bots. It is one clean ablation, one independent baseline, disciplined paired evaluation, and a fully validated submission.

Why this answer is tighter

I retained the recommendation’s strongest strategic insight, but narrowed the implementation to experiments that can realistically be coded, tested, and submitted within two hours.
